## The identity is the edge

Nobody in this league scored more than the Dark Knights this season. Fifty-four goals across 14 games, a league-best 3.86 goals per game, an offense that runs three lines deep, and a habit of getting *stronger* as games wear on — half of those 54 goals, 27 of them, came in the third period alone, more than double what this team scored in the first. That's not puck luck. That's a group that's still finishing hard while the rest of the league is gassed.

Christopher Godzyk was the best player in the Bronze division this year, period — the league's leading point-getter and the engine of everything the Knights do offensively. He's not doing it by shooting the lights out, either: just 4 goals against 12 assists. Godzyk is a table-setter first, which means the Knights' offense runs through his vision as much as anyone's finish. But what makes this team dangerous isn't just Godzyk, it's who's behind him. Jaylen Fast and Devan Baldwin both cracked double-digit points with real shooting numbers, and Jeffrey Hanson and Todd Ryan give the Knights secondary scoring most Bronze teams simply don't have. Wild has two real threats to worry about, Barajas and Oleary. The Knights have four.

That depth matters enormously against a Wild team that plays a tight, low-event game. If Wild wants to slow this series down, the Knights have more than enough firepower to make them pay for every mistake, from any line.

## Job one: put a leash on Barajas and Oleary

Two names decide this series more than any others. Brandon Barajas leads Wild with 13 goals on the season — a pure shooter, 13 goals against just one assist, so the goal isn't to bait a pass, it's to take away his shot. Michael Oleary is right behind him with 7 goals and 9 points, a legitimate second finisher who keeps this from being a one-man scouting report. Take away Barajas's shot and leave Oleary a step of space, and Wild still has an answer.

The head-to-head record makes the case for containment: in four meetings with the Knights this season, Barajas scored in three, including a three-goal night in the first matchup back in May. The one game the Knights held him off the board entirely, on June 2nd, they won. Barajas has taken just two penalties all season and Oleary only three, so neither is likely to get baited into a bad one — containing them has to come from positioning and shift-long shadowing, not from hoping either one loses his cool.

## The number that should worry the room

Here's the uncomfortable part: no team in the Bronze division spent more time in the box this season than the Knights did — 22 times shorthanded, more than any other team in the field. That's not a mystery — Dawne Griffith alone accounted for a significant chunk of it, taking 5 penalties of her own — the most on the team, though she also leads the Knights with 3 game-winning goals. The penalty kill has been outstanding all year, 90.9%, second in the division behind only Red Squadron's 94.4%, and that's exactly why the Knights have gotten away with it. But "getting away with it" isn't a plan for a playoff series against a team that plays as clean a game as Wild does.

The math is simple: Wild finished the season 0-for-20 on the power play, a 0.0% conversion rate. That is, by a wide margin, the softest special-teams unit in the division — nobody else in the Bronze field is below 66.7% on the kill — but playoff hockey has a way of being exactly when a cold streak ends. If the Knights keep taking bad penalties and Wild's power play wakes up at the wrong moment, a game the Knights should win on paper turns into a series decided by one power-play goal in the third period of a one-goal game.

The ask for this team next week isn't to stop being physical or to stop playing on the edge — it's what's made them who they are. It's about picking spots. Discipline in the final five minutes of a period. No penalties in the first thirty seconds after a whistle. The Knights don't need to become a different team to win this series. They need to be the same team with better habits for three periods.

## What a win looks like

This series comes down to a version of the same question in every period: does the Dark Knights' offense get to play its game at 5-on-5, or does undisciplined penalty trouble hand a scoreless Wild power play the one thing it hasn't had all year — a reason to believe in itself?

If the Knights play their identity — deep scoring, relentless pressure that builds as the game goes — keep Barajas and Oleary off the score sheet, and keep the penalty minutes to a manageable number, this is a team with more than enough talent to beat Wild in a series and set up a shot at Red Squadron in the final. The tools are all here. The season already proved that. Next week is about discipline meeting the talent that's been there all along.
